You are a reporter. Your job is to find out what really happened in the 1920s.

Every reporter covers one story. Reporters call the story they cover their "beat." Your beat is the topic you picked from the list.

Use this page with your assignment sheet and your Cornell Notes.

How a reporter works

This page will help you find all the information you need for this project.

  1. Pick your beat.
  2. Read about it. Start with the library databases below.
  3. Find five good quotes. Write them in your Cornell Notes.
  4. Explain each quote in your own words.
  5. Share your story with the class.

Library Databases

Britannica School

Start here.

Britannica is an encyclopedia. It gives you the big picture of your topic.

Every article comes in three reading levels: 1, 2, and 3. Look for the reading level buttons at the top of the article. Pick the one that feels right. You can switch any time.

Pop Culture Universe

Best for music, cars, and sports.

Pop culture means the things lots of people enjoy, like songs, games, and movies.

Do not start with the search box. Look under "Explore by Time Periods" and click on 1920s. Read the overview first. Then find the category on the left that best fits your topic.

Open Pop Culture Universe (opens in a new tab)

Explora

Best for magazine and newspaper articles.

Try to find a topic overview to get started. Topic overviews are short and easy to read. Then, try to find some articles.

Beat

Prohibition

The big question: Why did America ban alcohol? Did the ban work?

Word to know: An amendment is a change to the Constitution. The 18th Amendment banned alcohol.

Search words

  • Prohibition
  • 18th Amendment or Eighteenth Amendment
  • speakeasy
  • bootlegging
  • Volstead Act

Try these searches

  • "Prohibition" AND 1920s
  • speakeasy OR bootlegging

Websites to visit

  • Prohibition: An Interactive History (opens in a new tab) (The Mob Museum).
    A museum in Las Vegas made this site. Try the trivia!
  • Prohibition and Its Consequences (opens in a new tab) (DocsTeach, National Archives Foundation).
    Real letters, photos, and cartoons from the time.

Dig deeper: Who wanted Prohibition? Who fought against it?

Beat

The Tulsa Race Massacre (1921)

The big question: What happened in Greenwood in 1921?

A note before you start: This story is about real people who were hurt and killed. Some photos are hard to look at. It is okay to take a break. You can talk to Ms. Pecorino or Mr. Thompson if you're uncomfortable.

Word to know: A massacre is when many people who cannot defend themselves are killed.

Search tip: Older sources often call this the "Tulsa race riot." Newer sources say "massacre." Search for both. Why do you think the name changed?

Search words

  • Tulsa Race Massacre
  • Tulsa race riot
  • Greenwood
  • Black Wall Street
  • 1921 AND Oklahoma

Try these searches

  • "Tulsa Race Massacre" OR "Tulsa race riot"
  • Greenwood AND "Black Wall Street"

Websites to visit

  • The 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre (opens in a new tab) (Oklahoma Historical Society).
    Photos, videos, and newspapers from 1921. Some photos are upsetting.
  • Tulsa collection (opens in a new tab) (Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture).
    Watch survivors tell their own stories.

Dig deeper: What was Greenwood like before 1921?

Reporter's Kit

Search Like a Reporter

  • Use quotation marks to keep words together: "Model T"
  • AND finds both words. Use it to get fewer, better results.
  • OR finds either word. Use it for words that mean the same thing.
  • Try older words and words we use today. People in 1921 used different words than we do.
  • Too many results? Add a word.
  • Too few results? Take a word away.
  • Stuck? Ask Mr. Thompson.

How to Pick a Good Quote

A good quote:

  • is about your topic.
  • has a fact: a name, a date, a number, or an event.
  • makes sense to you. Read it out loud. If it confuses you, pick another one.
  • is short: one or two sentences.

When you copy it:

  • Copy it word for word.
  • Put it inside quotation marks.
  • Write down where it came from right away.

How to cite it (MLA):

After the quote, put the author's last name in parentheses.

No author? Use the title of the article in quotation marks. Shorten it if it is long.

A group can be the author, too: (HISTORY.com Editors).

Example: At trial, "the players were found not guilty" ("Black Sox Scandal").

Tip: Many databases have a Cite button. Choose MLA.

Can I Trust This Website?

Ask four questions:

  1. Who made it? Look for a museum, library, college, government (.gov), or news group.
  2. When was it made? Look for a date.
  3. Does it match? Find the same fact in a second source.
  4. Is it trying to sell you something or make you angry? Be careful.

Not sure? Ask Mr. Thompson.
